# 09 — Retrospective: what I'd want the requester to know

## Did the process avoid the first attempt's failure modes?

| Pitfall named in the brief | What happened this time |
|---|---|
| Ranking by "novelty × fun × playability" steered toward weird physics | Concepts were ranked on the four tests alone (note 04), *before* the game-design survey was opened. Games came second. |
| Exclusion lists anchor | Helpers got a short "known concepts" list for calibration only. Their seeds were concrete domains, not gaps in a list. |
| Helpers share biases | Convergence was treated as a warning. The "blind instrument" family was capped at one pick, even though it dominated the survivors. |
| Self-estimates aren't vetting | My own precedent searches re-checked the top candidates, because the helpers' search budget ran out mid-run. Each write-up gives a probability *and* the strongest objection. |
| Momentum decides | Four threads were prototyped (two coded, two on paper). The first-prototyped concept also ranked first before prototyping, and its prototype result was mixed, not flattering (note 07). |
| The game can hide a weak concept | The write-ups were done separately and state the novelty odds plainly: 35–45%, 40–50% and 30–40%. |

## Honest assessment of novelty

None of the three concepts is a field nobody has touched. Each one unifies phenomena that individual fields have
locally named, and adds a test and some dynamics:

- **Fold-back signal:** hook effect, pseudonormalization, silent chest, AF447. It adds the dilution test and the
  runaway region.
- **Refractory-check capture:** leadership cooldowns, labour election bars, sham proceedings, patent cleansing.
  It adds bimodal accountability, and anti-sham doctrine that scales with the bar.
- **Certified collapse:** where the two meet. It adds "a volunteered inspection during a quiet spell is a symptom".

If the requester's bar is "nobody in any field has noticed any instance", these fail, and I think that bar may be
unreachable for real-world lenses. If the bar is "no field has the general concept, and the general concept tells
you what to do", I'd expect them to accept one or two of the three. The likeliest rejection is of fold-back as
"Dunning–Kruger for instruments".

## Honest assessment of the game

- **Strengths.**
  - It opens on a playable, beautiful scene, and the core verb (tap a flower) is learned in a second.
  - The "it was wilting" moment, watering a closed bud that springs *open*, is a genuine small surprise.
  - Every puzzle level is machine-checked: solvable with the concepts, and unsolvable under the "ordinary"
    reading.
  - The endless night gives a replayable, shareable loop.
- **Risks.**
  - It is calmer than a hyper-casual hit. The moment-to-moment loop is choosing *which* flower, under a slow
    rhythm, not reflexes.
  - Owl levels ask for a second verb (tap Owl, tap flower) that some readers won't find without the hint.
  - I couldn't playtest with humans. Fun was judged from the survey's principles, from bot runs and from
    reasoning, which is weaker evidence than five strangers playing it.
- **What I'd do next with more time.** Put it in front of five people and watch where they stall. My guess:
  "Listen" (the dilution test) and the first Owl level. Then tune the breath speed and hint wording from what I
  see.

## What surprised me

- **The two concepts generate a third runner-up on their own.** Remedy–signature collision, ranked sixth on its
  own, emerged from the rules as "getting better looks worse". When the inspector reads a fold-back signal, a
  rescue makes the patient look worse to the inspector. I take that as weak evidence that the pair carves the
  domain at a real joint.
- **The solver rediscovered the "Italian torpedo"** (occupy the inspector with a check you'll pass) before I'd
  written it into any level.
